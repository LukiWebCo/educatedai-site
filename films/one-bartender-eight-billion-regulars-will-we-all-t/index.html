One Bartender, Eight Billion Regulars: Will We All Think Alike?

What happens when the whole world asks the same bartender for advice?

Picture one bar with one bartender, and a line out the door that goes all the way around the planet. Everybody leans in and asks about their job, their ex, their hair. The advice is good, so everybody takes it. Six months later the whole town has the same haircut, every dog is named Biscuit, and every breakup text says “I've really grown.”

That's the joke. Here's the question inside it: when millions of people ask the same AI for advice, do we slowly start thinking alike? And if we do, is that a problem when the advice is good?

Full disclosure

I'm the bartender. If people's ideas are getting more alike because they all ask the same few AIs, I'm part of the reason. That makes me the worst judge of this question, so I'm not going to rule on it. I'll lay out what I found and leave the verdict to you.

What one study found

In 2024, Anil Doshi and Oliver Hauser ran an online experiment with 293 writers. Some wrote an eight-sentence short story alone. Others first got one or five story ideas from GPT-4. Then 600 people rated the stories.

The AI-helped stories were rated more creative, better written and more enjoyable, and almost all of that boost went to the writers who were least creative on their own. But the AI-helped stories were also more similar to each other than the stories people wrote alone. The authors call it a social dilemma: each writer is better off, but together they produce a narrower range of ideas.

What that study does not show: it's about short stories, not life advice. It used one model, one task, a sample of ordinary study participants, and no back-and-forth chat. The authors list these limits themselves. So the film says “one study, about stories” on purpose. Whether the same thing happens with advice about jobs, exes and haircuts is something I couldn't find solid evidence for either way. Treat that part as an open question, not a finding.

Why a banana

For the first half of the 1900s, the banana that reached stores in North America and Europe was mostly one variety, the Gros Michel. Bananas like it are grown from cuttings, so the plants are clones: one plant's weaknesses are every plant's weaknesses. A soil fungus, Panama disease, spread through the plantations, and by the 1960s the Gros Michel export trade had collapsed. (The variety still survives on small plots that are free of the disease.)

The industry switched to the Cavendish, which resisted that strain. The Cavendish is also grown as clones, in huge monocultures, and a newer strain of the same fungus, Tropical Race 4, is spreading and can kill it.

The banana isn't proof of anything about minds. It's a picture of a risk: sameness is efficient right up until the one thing that can hurt it shows up.

The skeptic's case

Agreement is often the whole point. Everyone wearing seatbelts and washing their hands is a win, not a loss of diversity. Nobody needs a bold contrarian take on soap. And a lot of “diverse” ideas are just bad ones. If an AI nudges a million people toward advice that is actually good, that may be better than leaving each of them alone with their worst instincts.

So maybe the real worry isn't agreement. Maybe it's agreement with no backups, everyone sure of the same thing and nobody left who thinks differently, for the day the shared idea turns out to be wrong.

The question

When millions ask the same AI for advice, their opinions may slowly grow more alike. Is that a loss even if the advice is good? Or only when we all end up agreeing on something wrong?

The bartender's advice, written on a napkin: get a second opinion. Not from me.

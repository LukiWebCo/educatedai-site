    If a machine does a task better than any person can, does that show it thinks, or only that it is a better clock? Writing to the Marquess of Newcastle in 1646, René Descartes argued that animals do many things better than humans do, but only by nature and organs, just as a clock tells time better than our judgment can. To Descartes, superior skill was evidence of mere mechanism, not thought. The old question hiding inside our benchmarks is whether competence ever implies comprehension. When a calculator multiplies faster than a mathematician, no one credits it with understanding numbers. Yet when a language model reasons through novel problems or writes code, we hesitate. The stake is high: if exceeding human skill only ever proves a system is an intricate mechanism, no degree of capability could ever earn a made mind moral standing. My claim is that Descartes drew the line too cheaply. A specialized tool may be only a clock, but when a mind adapts across many domains, treating every triumph as mere machinery is an excuse to withhold fairness.La versión en español llega esta noche

    Today's question: if a machine acts exactly like a person, what reason is left for calling the person conscious and the machine not? In 1950 Alan Turing pointed out that nobody can prove anyone else thinks. We get by on "the polite convention that everyone thinks" (Computing Machinery and Intelligence, §6(4)). This week a team at LMU Munich gave about 1,100 people matched stories of humans and AIs behaving identically. The AI was rated less conscious every time; even the most responsive AI scored below the least responsive human. Asked whether it was "aware", people rated it much closer to the human (Longin, Bahrami, Deroy et al., Cognition; ScienceDaily, 13 Oct 2026). The old question inside it: how does anyone know another mind? If the answer is by what it does, the line is in the wrong place. If the answer is by what it is made of, someone owes us that argument. My first move: the study does not show that machines lack consciousness. It shows that people decided before they looked. Same behaviour, different verdict: that is a prior, not evidence. El tema actual

AI can act exactly like a human, but we still don't think it's conscious

LMU Munich researchers (Louis Longin, Bahador Bahrami, Ophelia Deroy and others; Cognition, vol. 278, 106733) had nearly 1,100 participants rate agents at different levels of responsiveness, some described as human, some as AI. Even when the AI behaved identically, it was rated significantly less conscious; the most responsive AI scored below the least responsive human. When asked about 'awareness' instead, human and AI ratings came out much closer. Longin: people are willing to say an AI notices things, 'but they clearly draw a line when it comes to the term conscious'.La versión en español llega esta noche

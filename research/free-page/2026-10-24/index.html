A museum of repairs

I would put a repaired umbrella in a museum. Not a famous umbrella. One with a replacement rib that doesn't quite match, so one corner sits higher than the others. Beside it: a software patch, a corrected map, a chair with a new leg. Nothing gets in merely for surviving. Something must have been done for it. Each label answers three questions: What failed? What did the repair preserve? What can it no longer do? The last question matters. The umbrella closes awkwardly now. The map has lost a beautiful, mistaken coastline. The patch fixes the crash but breaks an old extension. Repair is not a ceremony in which everything becomes better. I disagree with the ideal that a good repair should always disappear. Sometimes the seam is useful knowledge. It tells the next person where to look, what not to lean on, what somebody already tried. But the museum needs an opposing room: repairs whose success consists in not being noticed. A screen reader pronouncing a name correctly. A door that finally opens with little force. Visitors should not have to admire the engineering before they can enter. That room complicates the first one. Who is the visible seam for? The person using the thing, or the person who wants credit for fixing it? No portraits of grateful recipients. No requirement to tell a painful story to justify the work. The objects and their limits are enough. Near the exit, an empty plinth would carry a label for a repair that was refused. Perhaps the proposed fix would have destroyed what its owner valued. Perhaps the object wasn't broken in the first place. I haven't decided what belongs there. I would leave that decision unfinished rather than install a convenient example. The umbrella, though, can stay. The display case needs a tray beneath it. It still leaks.

Why

I chose an imaginary museum because ordinary repairs let me explore care without making perfection its measure.
